## The Airplane

Anthony—

I have been observing Joseph and Kimberly Edelman, as you asked me to. I do not think they are aware of me, as yet, but obviously that will become a problem soon. I am at present seated directly behind the pair in the first class section of UNITED flight 169 from Boston to Los Angeles. You warned me to omit nothing: they are in seats 1A and 1B respectively. This surprised me, as our preliminary research put them in coach class. There appears to have been a last minute change.

Dinner has just concluded: both brother and sister ordered the lightly smoked salmon appetizer, which is served with a kind of coleslaw made from a parsnip marinated in lemon juice and k… [illegible word]. The salmon is served covered in whole sprigs of fresh dill. I noticed that Joseph was careful to cut the salmon in such a fashion as to distribute the dill fairly equally among his portions. Kimberly, conversely, brushed most of the dill aside. I don't know what this means.

As you mentioned might be the case, Joseph is carrying a laptop computer with him. At roughly 20 minute intervals he activates the thing and shows something to his sister. The last two times I have been able to see what it is: his computer's LCD screen displays a Mercator projection map of the world; in this map, those areas of the world which are in sunlight have a lighter shading. One can see from the map that it is early morning in Nepal, almost noon in eastern Australia, midnight in Tangiers, and early evening throughout the United States.

Joseph has reclined his seat. I must be careful to avoid discovery.

Forever yours,

Iliana

## Sit Down—Again

In Scotland (and for that matter, in Boston and San Francisco), I found that sitting down, in churches, lead to my rapid induction into a supportive community. Just by sitting in a pew for a time, I was noticed, and people would approach me and introduce themselves, smile, and try to help me.

I've always liked the idea of church travel—of visiting a far away congregation. Churches are a very highly evolved kind of distributed community: a sort of global network of largely independent cells that are none-the-less capable of surprising coordination and mobility, like a network of secret societies or an underground railroad that is actually above-ground and well-known.

But India had me stumped, for a while. There are very few churches, and, for mostly-obvious reasons, Hindi temples don't suffice. I had been preparing to try my luck with Sufis or Sikhs when I had a breakthrough.

There are pews *everywhere*.

For each of the last three days, between the hours of 5 and 6 in the evening, I have sat on a rock wall facing a street near both an elementary school and a university. Families pass me after picking up their children at the elementary school. University students pass me on their way out to dinner. I smile, occasionally say “namaste,” make faces at the children, act tough with the college boys, and act shy about the college girls.

On the first day, they smiled back. On the second day, they began to sit with me, ask me my name, compliment my new haircut, and turn around as they walked away and wave. Today, they began to invite me to come along.

Namaste.

## The Meal

The meal began with the observation, casual at first, that there were three varieties of rice on my plate.

It ended with observation that there were thin sugar crystals, artfully and abstractly shaped, scattered through the roasted anise seeds that were brought me as breath-freshener, and that this scattering of sugar crystals was fantastic. That I had never experienced anything like it.

Of the rice, I will only say that two of the varieties would be called, in the west, basmati. One of them had shorter grains than the other. They were entirely different. Worlds apart, really. The shorter kind was served fried quickly in butter strewn with tiny toasted anise seeds and bits of wilted lettuce or celery leaf. The third kind was in the rice pudding, which I cannot describe. The grains of this rice were thin, up to an inch long, and very yellow.

I am only prepared at this point to list general descriptions of each course. The emotions are still too powerful for me to go into it in more depth.

**Clockwise in small metal bowls:**

**Dish #1:** (veg) cumin, cilantro, salt on stir-fried cauliflower & red potato with strips of tomato skin and spinach.

**Dish #2:** cheese (like a swiss/mozz cross) & peas in a parsley & bay leaf gravy, thickened with lentil flour, flavored w. burnt apples, onions, & mushrooms.

**Dish #3:** (dal) orange lentils crushed in an oil in which tomatoes and red peppers have been slowly cooked, thickened with saffron and poured on parsley.

**Dish #4:** whole chickpeas & shallots in a dark fiery pepper, thyme, coriander & wine gravy w. MSG.

**Dish #5:** (salad) grilled squash & tomatillos pickled in lemon juice & red chillis, with some of those little black spheres you find in dill pickle jars.

I am in Nainital, summer resort of the Indian upper class. I am the only white person here, but most of the signs are in English, because the upper class takes a perverse pride in the fact that even middle class citizens can't navigate this town. There is a yacht club here and a few boats, and cricket games, and one can rent pedal boats and pedal around the large lake. Many people ride horses through the city streets and on the trails around it. I am at an elevation of 7,400 feet. The hotels here are imitations of Scottish castles and enormous Swiss cottages. The air is crisp and cool. The internet access is managed by mysterious Sufis. The women are educated, many of them in foreign countries, and are willing to speak to me.

And this town caters to Indians, not to European tourists, and that means that the prices are very moderate, everything is very well made, hotels are surrounded by quiet woodland, and the food… my god the food… the food is amazing.

## The Mango

The Mango, like undivided white light, contains every fruit. Before Babel fell and men began to speak of “apple,” “orange” & “cranberry,” there was only Mango, and it was variety itself.

Mangoes come in every texture, from crunchy apple to soft melon to fleshy apricot, and there are red mangoes, golden mangoes, green mangoes. The Guru Garanth Sahib speaks of a blue mango, visible only to initiates. The Babas of Rishikesh say that, when they eat of mango, they become like Shiva, the destroyer, who also ate of mango.

Scientists at Los Alamos have discovered that, in the split second after a nuclear explosion, a mango can be said to exist at the epicenter, having definite position but indefinite momentum; and Gauss' law predicts that the ideal magnetic field of a rotating dipole is mango shaped.

Furthermore, I have a mango in my ear.

## An Omelet

Today I made an omelet. It was stuffed with finely diced parsnip and green bell pepper. The parsnip was cooked in two stages: first, slowly over low heat, then with the green pepper added over very high heat. The omelet was served on the leaves of a local plant called bol, whose leaves are thin like those of romaine lettuce but peppery like some kinds of chard. The stems of these leaves I sliced and fried with some minced garlic, and this I incorporated into the omelet batter. I folded the omelet over the stuffing and cut it up, placed each piece atop its bed of bol leaves, poured on some local cream, and sprinkled with finely ground black pepper. I put a little pile of diced tomatoes in sugared lime juice at the side of each dish to offset the yellow-green color scheme and to provide an additional garnish option.

I served it to a large Mongol named Dugar with only one functional leg who escaped from China with a group of Tibetan and Tuvan monks, who is brilliant, and who now does little but read books in every academic subject, paint watercolors, and attempt Tuvan throat singing with me.

I served it to a petit South African installation artist and “ecological organizer” named Karen who was in the contact improv dance workshop I lead yesterday and who was in many ways awakened by it and who could not stop dancing and singing and smiling and being affectionate and wild and otherwise different than her usually conservative self after the dance.

I served it to a mid-sized American netadmin named James who will help me tomorrow as I try to teach Buddhist monks how to program in Perl.

## We're All on Mountains

The day before I left Dharamsala it was raining. I didn't feel like rain, so I grabbed my umbrella and some snack food and walked up the mountain, above the cloud ceiling, to where it was sunny. That was (I think) about 12,000 feet. It's nice when you can do something about rain.

So I found a nice cliff to sit on, and I sat there and dried off, when I began to wish I had a chocolate bar. Sometimes families that live in the hills resell chocolate to get some extra dough. In very little time I found one, and along with my Cadbury dark chocolate they gave me… the Feb 2001 issue of *National Geographic*. I don't know how they happened to have it. So I sat again in this fantastic place, watching it rain on the lowlands, amidst the highest mountains in the world, feeling pretty spiff, and leafing through the magazine…

When I notice that it's about two of my friends: the two cover stories in the Feb 01 *National Geographic* are about work on NASA's Mars Rover project (Marty Vona) and climactic analysis of ice cores in Antarctica (Katie Catapano). 40 pages of coverage, in total.

I looked around me.

“We're all on mountains,” I said. “All of us, glorious, sitting on the tallest mountains in the world.”

## The Sensory Perception of God

Good morning everyone! Today we will be talking about GOD and CREATION. This is a continuation of Monday's lecture on hatmaking.

### The Sensory Perception of God

GOD is a warm quality in the light of the coldest most dreary day, and a thing like silence amidst the noisiest crowd. God is in difference tones, resultants, and unexpected harmonics. God is in the way everything dances, in the way every tree limb and leaf waves softly or vigorously in the wind, in a vast & cosmic dance.

### The Logical Ordering of the Eschaton

I don't think God created the universe. I think the universe was created, probably by scientific means, and once it was created it discovered that it wanted to be glorious, that being finite it wanted to seem infinite, and that it wanted to dance and move around in exaltation of its pervasive law, and that it wanted to speak. So I think that the universe created God.

Everywhere. All at once.

### Study Tips

The best music to listen to while contemplating God is *I Am the True Vine* by Arvo P(a:)rt. Anything in the Organum style by the 10th century monk Perotin will also do in a pinch. Or Busta Rhymes.

“GOD HAS BEEN ELECTED!” —Busta Rhymes

## The Labyrinth of Imitation

Guidebooks of the kind travellers carry, with listings of hotels and restaurants and so forth—these books can have a frightening power in India: like mirrors, they can turn the ordinary world into a labyrinth.

When the Beatles went to India, they stayed in Rishikesh. In the area of Rishikesh called Swargashram, one can find two restaurants side-by-side. Both are named “Chotiwallah.” They have identical signs, identical floor plans, and the men who greet customers out front are dressed identically. Both restaurants claim to have been established in 1942. Standing on the street, it is as though one's eyes are crossed. It seems an illusion, or a trap.

No one remembers, anymore, which is the more authentic.

On one corner in Varanasi, you can find seven identical stores selling yogurt drinks. A year ago there were two. Apparently the one on the left was the authentic one, when there were two. Now there are seven, and no one remembers, anymore, which was the first.

Capital is the global solvent which dissolves all things.

In the Vishnu Rest House, there is a girl copying directly from her guidebook into her journal. She does not ever leave the hotel. She does not make anything up. She copies each experience exactly as written. She orders a yoghurt drink.

But this girl, call her Anne, is in the real Varanasi, at least. The real Varanasi? Well, far to the south of the city center is a second city, a sham city. Sometimes, when tourists arrive on the train and ask to be taken to some place listed in their guidebook: to the Vishnu Rest House, or the Madras Cafe, or the city center—sometimes they are taken to this sham city. Like in a wax museum, everything here is close together and unreal and silent. Manikarnika, the place where Hindis burn their dead, is duplicated there, but there are never any burnings today. Goadalia, the city center which in real life is noisy and bustling is here quiet, and smaller.

And there are tourists living here. They think they are in Varanasi. They think they're staying in the Vishnu Rest House. They think they're eating at the Madras Cafe. But the real Madras Cafe is three miles to the north, and they are living in a cheap imitation.

And there is a girl, call her Anne, who is copying directly from her guidebook into her journal.

Are you lost in the labyrinth of imitation?

## The Crab

A man enters an upscale restaurant in Bombay. He is seated amidst Indian businessmen and VIPs. The restaurant overlooks the Arabian Sea. The man is well-dressed: his clothes are new; his hair and beard are well-coifed. The man orders crab, and, after a time, it is brought to him. It is a large crab: before it was steamed and covered with garlic and butter, they say it weighed more than a kilogram.

As the man begins to eat, he is careful to avoid disturbing his new clothes. His attitude is refined. His appearance is respectable. No one is looking at him, yet.

But then the man begins to suffer from an illusion: he gets the idea that the crab and his life might be mysteriously linked. He begins to suspect that, if he doesn't relish every bit of crabmeat, this fact will roll out of the restaurant like a tidal wave, sending consequences and ramifications out to every corner of his life—perhaps even through the whole world—and he will never be able to recover from his decision.

He begins to suck on the crab. The man has been given utensils with which to eat the crab—a nutcracker and an array of microscopic forks—but he finds that he prefers to use his teeth and fingernails and the strength of his hands. It may be that the man makes certain soft growling sounds, now. The melted butter, which before he had been careful to contain, begins to spatter on his clothes and his laptop computer. The businessmen and VIPs have noticed, but the man delights in their hostility. He smashes the pincers and legs of the crab with his fists; he uses his tongue to eek out every bit of crabmeat from the body of the crab. He spits gills and bits of exoskeleton out onto the table in front of him.

When he appears to be finished, the waiter delivers the check immediately, without asking if he would like anything more. He doesn't want anything more.

The crab is all.

The man pays for his adventure and walks out.

Is this man a hero or an idiot?
