The audience-- mouths gaping, stomachs growling-- crowds around one
side of the table.  The chow-mein chefs queue up on the other.  It is
impossible to say which group is more excited.

The wok *gleams*.

The gas flame *roars*.

The resulting chowmein is *pretty bad*.

First comes the master.  A shudder goes down his arm, into the wok,
and a pound of sliced cabbage flies into the air, does a 3/4 backwards
half-summersault, and lands, without disturbing the oil, back in the wok.
Members of the audience glance at each other:  "did you see that?"
Especially excited are the man and his wife (it's a big night, tonight)
who get to eat it.  She looks at him, her love renewed.

Next comes his best student.  His hair is oiled and swept back.
He's wearing a leather belt (unheard of!).  But he glances at the master
nervously, and tries flipping the noodles.  It goes well.  The master
is pleased.

Behind are three or four more of the master's disciples.  In India it
is possible to study anything.  It is a country rich in instruction, and
poor in critical evaluation.  The relationship of master and student is
a defined one and penetrates all aspects of life.  I am sure that The
Performance Chow-Mein Chefs of Uttar Pradesh address their eldest as
"father" and that he sits on the highest pillow.

And mainstream India is not interested in taste, comfort, or subtlety.
This India is interested in spectacle.  Their religion is spectacular,
full of vast rivers, big fires, enormous sculptures of Gods in various
poses of power and kingdom, and magic tricks.  Their cities are
spectacular, lit by halogen floodlights and a million pilgrims a day.
And in Uttar Pradesh, their chow-mein noodles are spectacular, turning
in the air under bright white light and the eyes of an adoring crowd.
The taste?  I don't think anyone pays any attention!  Focus instead on
the spiritual infusion of spectacle and mastery-- the hoopla which sits
beyond the taste and connects with the hoopla of the universe.

	Joe
